Part 1: Energy Sources

Energy is the ability to do work or cause change. Humans use energy to heat homes, move vehicles, grow food, make products, and power technology.

Nonrenewable Energy

These resources form over millions of years or are limited in supply. Examples include coal, oil, natural gas, and uranium for nuclear power.

  • Usually reliable and energy-dense
  • Can create air pollution, greenhouse gases, mining impacts, or radioactive waste
  • Often used for transportation, heating, and electricity

Renewable / Alternative Energy

Renewable energy comes from sources that are naturally replenished, including solar, wind, hydropower, geothermal, and biomass.

  • Often lower pollution while operating
  • Some depend on weather, location, or time of day
  • May need batteries, transmission lines, or backup systems

Key facts to know

EIA explains that renewable energy includes biomass, hydropower, geothermal, wind, and solar, and that renewables provided about 21% of U.S. electricity generation in 2023. EIA also reported that U.S. solar and wind production increased in 2024 as new generators came online.

Part 2: Water Resources, Use, and Waste

Only a small part of Earth’s water is usable freshwater. Humans need clean water for drinking, sanitation, farming, electricity, and manufacturing.

Human Use

Homes, schools, hospitals, businesses, firefighting, parks, and public systems all require reliable water.

Agriculture

Crops and livestock need water. Some water is returned to rivers or groundwater, but some is consumed by plants, animals, and evaporation.

Industry

Factories and power plants use water for cooling, cleaning, processing materials, and generating electricity.

Key facts to know

USGS identifies crop irrigation, thermoelectric power generation, and public supply as the top three U.S. water-use categories. EPA also emphasizes that communities need water for homes, local business, industry, firefighting, parks, and public systems.

Part 3: Agriculture Focus — Beef

Beef connects energy, water, land, climate, and waste. Cattle need water directly, but beef also requires water and energy to grow feed crops, transport animals, process meat, refrigerate food, and manage waste.

Environmental Questions

  • How much land and feed are needed?
  • Where does methane come from?
  • How can manure affect waterways?
  • What practices can reduce impact?

Balanced Thinking

Your answer should not be “beef is good” or “beef is bad.” A strong answer explains benefits, costs, tradeoffs, and possible improvements.

FAO notes that livestock products tend to produce more greenhouse gas emissions than most other food sources, with emissions coming from feed production, digestion, animal waste, and land-use change.

FAO Livestock & Climate

Part 4: Industrial Use and Waste

Industry uses water and energy to make products people buy every day: food, clothes, electronics, vehicles, paper, plastics, steel, and chemicals.

Industrial Water Uses

  • Cooling machines and power plants
  • Washing and cleaning materials
  • Mixing chemicals or ingredients
  • Transporting waste

Industrial Energy Uses

  • Heating, melting, and drying
  • Running motors and machines
  • Transporting materials
  • Lighting and electronics

A major hidden connection: many power plants use water for cooling. This means electricity choices can also affect water resources.
